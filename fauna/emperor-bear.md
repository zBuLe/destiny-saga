
# Emperor Bear 

---

"Emperor Bear" [a polar bear, feathered, white plumage with black back, yellow crest, blunt beak, talon paws, habitat: frigid prairie on top of a multi story snowpack] the apex predator of the snowfield. it can eat anything, and no one can make it think otherwise. if it finds a berry farm, consider it lost. it can easily break open a coconut. it can chase down but the fastest critters, able to keep up with a "dog horse". mating season is during the springtime. and pups are born during the summer. one or two per mother. the pups will follow the mother, fattening them up. during late fall, their most impressive feat happens. mothers will plunge from the shear cliff down the fierce waters, and basically disappear for a month. the fathers may or may not join their bulkier mate. either way, the pups stay at the cliffs edge, not moving, not hunting, not eating. they waste away. good thing the mother fatten them up beforehand. the fathers may end up abandoning the family, but not the mothers. nonetheless. for like a month, right before winter, the "emperor bear" hunt marine fauna far from the shore. the use glacier sheets for resting. but they spend most to the time hunting underwater. they are strong swimmers, able to power thru waters that break ships apart. bountiful schools of fish await them. if they are lucky, they may feast on a "whale" calf, or steal a whalers catch. for a month they eat as much as they can, bulk up, increase their blubber stores. once they are done, they return to the cliff the jumped off, and start climbing. it takes a few hours for them to climb. if they slip up, if they let go, they plunge back down the fierce waters, and at this point, may be too exhausted to successfully climb again. once they finish climbing, the mother reunites with her pups, slimer than they were before. but that is ok. for now she can feed them nutrient dense milk. one last week of hunting before the go to hibernate. the pups, from time to time, wake up and feed from their mother thru the hibernation. during the hibernation in the winter, is the only time the snowfield is safe from "emperor bears" they emerge back on early spring. once the pups are 5 years old, the mother chases them away and are on their own.

additional notes. "emperor bear" mixes traits from polar bears and emperor penguins while the "emperor bear" can keep up with a "dog horse" , the "dog horse" can out maneuver and outlast them, just needs to avoid getting caught before that happens. on their first year, the father may stay with the pups, protect and feed them a bit. the young pups stay glued to the cliff's edge, worrying about their mother for a month. by the fourth year, they know the mother would be fine, and roam a bit, but stay near the same spot. it is possible for a pup to fall, either they decide to jump or slip. they may not be strong enough to climb back. until the third year.

further additions: so for this aggressive mating cycle. the males seek out the females when they emerge from hibernation. their drive of mating is so aggressive, the female may still be hibernating. if she already has pups, she will completely reject the male. if she was woken up, she might reject the male that woke her up. if she woke up on her own, she is more receptive.

courtship may last a week. she must accept the male's advances. if there is no successful mating within the first two weeks of spring, there will be no cubs this year for her.

one or two pups are born late spring, early summer. newborns are very small. the mother carries her small pups on her back. the more receptive the mother was during the courtship, the more likely the father remains. the less receptive, the more likely he abandons the family. he may abandon the family at any time anyways. if both bears are together, the stronger their pups will be for the vigil. two bears can chase off any solitary bear. as long as they can find the food, it is theirs. because the priority is the pup's growth, the father may give up his share to the mother. as the more the mother eats, the more the pups eat. by the 2nd week they are big enough to get off the mothers back. but most dont do so until a month or a bit more.

the pups are very attached to their mother. she is their sole source of food. and her back is the best warmth available in the frigid snowfields. the father's back is a close second. if the pair are together with two pups. the strongest rides on the mothers back when they are too large for both. if it is just the mother, they alternate. but the strongest one gets to have more time. a single pup gets to have their best life, annoying whichever parent is stuck giving them a ride.

until the plunge. because of their hyper attachedness, wont leave the cliffs edge. if the father gave more food shares to the mother, thus he himself is underweight, he will dive. but their pups are stronger because of it. if they don't dive, they will patrol, keep their pups safe, and try to give them food. they had not yet eaten but their mothers milk. the pups may still not eat.

The "dog horse" is a very large and bulky dog used to pull sleds, the main form of transportation on the snowfield. So if the " emperor bear" is chasing a "dog horse" they are also chasing the people and cargo the "dog horse" is hauling. the "dog horse" can out maneuver and outlast them, just needs to avoid getting caught before that happens. For the month long feast, mothers always take the plunge, fathers may or may not. solitary bears, without pups, may or may not. while across the water many creatures are moving away from the soon to be cold winter waters. this makes easy for predators like the emperor bear to feast. but to even access that bounty, they have to transverse the fierce coastal waters.  
before the plunge, they need to take as much air into their lungs as they carry. to maximize the volume, they start roaring, to clear their airways. "Emperor Bears" can be seen lining up along the cliff edge roaring, preparing for the plunge. once their lungs are at capacity. they plunge. they jump off as far as they can. they streamline themselves midflight, so they enter the water with little resistance. they use the momentum to decent under the waves as fast as possible. the air they carry is not to hold their breath, as they actually release air from their nostril to cover themselves in air bubbles. this increases their speed. they go as far deep and away as they can. they also need to navigate the currents, some with push them back, others will propel them forward. they use their own bubbles as to judge the currents. eventually they have to go back up for air, or they will drown. before they asphyxiate, they break the thru the surface, back to the air. but this is not enough, they aren't far enough to clear the rough waters. they have to go underwater again. depending on factors, it may take between 3 to seven hops to reach calm waters and the feeding waters. the initial cliff dive gives them the most distance. a well feed mother can make it halfway, with two more hops. underweight fathers tend to struggle. and breach the water one third of the way. because of this, couples tend to separate. they may reunite in the feeding waters, if they both traveled to the same one. there are six feeding waters. two of which are accessed initially from the same currents before they diverge. as they prepare for the subsequent hops. the roar again as the bob on the wave, completely at their mercy. if there is a floating ice sheet nearby, or anything, they will take it. fill themselves with air again and dive underwater once more. exhaustion is another danger they face. the sooner they clear the coastal waters the better, but this exertion fatigues them faster. they may succumb and sink, unable to continue to swim. it is possible for the waters to push them back to the snowfield negating any distance they had made. because of the demanding travel, if a bare doesn't have to, most likely they won't, but the abundant food is still enticing to some. still, most will power thru winter with what they got. an underweight father has to consider if they can find enough food before winter, most likely not, or take the risk for the abundant food on the other side.
